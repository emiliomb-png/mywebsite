Do Rich Parents Have Rich Kids? An SQL Tutorial to Match Parents and Children in Data

income
intergenerational
Published

October 23, 2026

Since 1968, the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID) has followed the same families, and then their children’s families. That makes it perfect for asking how much a parent’s income shapes a child’s. The catch, however, is that nothing in the data says “this is Ana’s mother.” You have to find the parents yourself, and SQL inside Python can be a useful tool for this type of analysis. What follows is a brief tutorial of how to do this.

Step 1: Give everyone a name tag

Every person is identified by two numbers: their family’s 1968 interview number (id68) and a person number (pn). Family IDs change every wave, so these 1968 numbers are the only stable labels. Combine them into one name tag: id681000+pn. A separate Parent Identification file lists each person’s birth mother and father with the same two numbers, 0 meaning “none.” Load both into SQLite:

con = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
ind.to_sql("individuals", con, index=False)
pid.to_sql("parent_ids", con, index=False)

Step 2: Find each child’s parents

NULLIF turns the 0s into NULLs, so nobody matches a parent who doesn’t exist:

CREATE TABLE parent_keys AS
SELECT id68*1000+pn AS person_id,
       NULLIF(mother_id68,0)*1000+NULLIF(mother_pn,0) AS mother_id,
       NULLIF(father_id68,0)*1000+NULLIF(father_pn,0) AS father_id
FROM parent_ids;

Now the clever part, a self-join. Picture persons (built the same way from individuals) as a phone book. For each child we look up the mother’s entry and the father’s entry in the same book, using two aliases, m and f:

CREATE TABLE child_parent AS
SELECT pk.person_id AS child_id, pk.mother_id, pk.father_id
FROM parent_keys pk
LEFT JOIN persons m ON pk.mother_id = m.person_id
LEFT JOIN persons f ON pk.father_id = f.person_id;

LEFT JOIN means “keep the child even if the parent isn’t in the book”. An INNER JOIN would quietly erase them and bias the sample. Then check with GROUP BY and HAVING that no child appears twice:

SELECT child_id, COUNT(*) FROM child_parent GROUP BY child_id HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;

Zero rows means a clean match. Aggregation grades it too: SUM(mother_id IS NOT NULL) counts children with a known mother.

Step 3: Turn families into income

Income lives in yearly family files, and a family’s number changes every wave. So we reshape the wide individual file into one row per person per wave (UNION ALL), then JOIN on wave and family number to give each person-year their family’s income. Each income variable describes the previous calendar year, and we convert everything to 2016 dollars.

Parents are measured when the child was 15–19, and children at 30–40. One year, or many? For each person we rank observed years by closeness to a central age (17 for parents, 35 for children). Rank 1 is the snapshot. The average is the movie:

ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY child_id ORDER BY ABS(age - 35), age) AS rk
...
AVG(CASE WHEN rk = 1 THEN income END) AS single,  -- snapshot
AVG(income)                           AS multi    -- movie

GROUP BY child collapses the years into one row per child. We keep children with at least two parental years and three of their own, and drop incomes under $1,000 so logs work.

Step 4: How sticky is income?

Regress log child income on log parent income. The slope is the intergenerational elasticity (IGE): 0.46 means children of parents earning 10% more earn about 4.6% more.

fit = sm.OLS(np.log(d[y]), sm.add_constant(np.log(d[x]))).fit()

Swap a one-year parental snapshot for the movie and the estimate jumps by about 0.08. Averaging child income barely matters.

Step 5: When you take the photo matters

Now use child income at a single age (parents: multi-year; see table below):

Child age 25 30 35 40 45
IGE 0.428 0.419 0.512 0.452 0.550
Mean income $61,427 $70,254 $84,253 $90,567 $98,179

At 25–30 the estimate is about 0.42; by 35 and 45 it reaches 0.51 and 0.55. That is life-cycle bias: young adults are still starting out, so their income says less about where they will end up. The pattern isn’t smooth (age 40 dips) and each age mixes different cohorts, so treat it as a hint.

Step 6: Why the movie beats the snapshot

Imagine photographing a family in the year Dad got a bonus, or Mom was laid off. That photo is long-run status plus luck. Luck in the variable you regress on dilutes the relationship and pulls the slope toward zero, so single-year parental income understates the IGE. Averaging years washes out much of the luck and leaves something closer to a family’s standard of living.
